Plugs

Bring your European charger and one adapter with the UK-style three-pin plug (type G). That covers every country on the route.

Japanese and American chargers both use the flat two-pin plug (type A). European means the slim two-round-pin plug (type C). Chunky European plugs with side grounding clips (type F) won't fit everywhere. In the Philippines, older sockets take only flat pins, so a Japanese or American charger is the safer bet there.

Check the voltage on your Japanese charger

Japan runs on 100 V. Every country on this route runs on 220–240 V. Look at the small print on the charger: "INPUT 100–240V" means it works everywhere. If it says only 100 V, it will burn out the moment you plug it in. Almost all phone and laptop chargers are 100–240 V. Hair dryers and some shavers aren't.

The simplest setup

  • One USB-C charger with several ports (a GaN charger, 65 W or so) charges phone, laptop and power bank at once.
  • One universal travel adapter that does type G. Or buy a G adapter for a few ringgit at any 7-Eleven in Malaysia.
  • A power bank under 100 Wh (about 27,000 mAh). It goes in your carry-on, never in checked bags. Several Asian airlines, including AirAsia, Thai and Vietnam Airlines, now ban using power banks during flights, so charge before boarding.

SIM & data

Check your phone is unlocked and supports eSIM before you go.

  • The easy option: one regional Asia eSIM (Airalo, Holafly or Saily) covering all seven countries. Set it up at home and it works the moment you land. It costs more per GB than a local SIM.
  • The cheap option: a local SIM per country, bought at the airport or a 7-Eleven. Thailand: AIS or True. Vietnam: Viettel (best coverage in the mountains). Laos: Unitel. Cambodia: Smart. Malaysia: CelcomDigi or Hotlink. Philippines: Smart or Globe (registration with your passport required).
  • The middle way: the regional eSIM as a backup, plus local SIMs in Thailand and Vietnam where you stay longest.
  • Keep your Swedish SIM in for bank and BankID codes, but turn off data roaming on it.

Money

  • Wise or Revolut card for good exchange rates, plus a backup card kept in a different bag.
  • Thailand: ATMs charge about 220 baht per withdrawal whatever the amount, so take out more each time.
  • Laos: cash country. ATMs are few and limits are low, so arrive with kip or dollars.
  • Vietnam: cash for street food and small places, cards in cities.
  • Cambodia: runs on US dollars. ATMs give dollars, change comes in riel.
  • Malaysia, Singapore: cards and tap-to-pay almost everywhere.
  • Philippines: cash on Palawan. El Nido and Coron ATMs run dry, so bring enough for the Tao trip.
  • Always choose to be charged in local currency when a card machine asks.

Worth packing

  • A proper warm layer and gloves for Ha Giang in December (5–15°C, colder on a bike) and the northern Thai mornings.
  • A light rain jacket for Hue, Hoi An and Kuala Lumpur.
  • A dry bag for boat days and the Tao expedition.
  • A rash vest for snorkelling. It beats sunscreen on your back.
  • Reef-safe sunscreen, which some marine parks require.
  • A padlock for hostel lockers, a headlamp, and a quick-dry towel.
  • Long trousers and covered shoulders for temples and the Grand Palace.
  • Pack light and buy clothes on the way. Bangkok, Chiang Mai and Hoi An are where you'll want the space.
